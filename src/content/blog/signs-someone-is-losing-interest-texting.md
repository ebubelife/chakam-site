"They just seem different lately" is the most common way this conversation starts, and it's also the least useful. Vibes are real, but they're not evidence — and when you're trying to figure out if someone's losing interest, going purely on a feeling is how you either panic over nothing or miss something real for weeks.

The more reliable signal isn't *how* someone sounds. It's *what changed* — measured against how they actually used to text you, not against some universal standard of "a good texter." Here's what to actually look at.

## 1. Reply time drifting, not just being slow

A slow replier who's always been a slow replier isn't losing interest — that's just who they are. What matters is the *trend*: someone who used to text back in minutes and now takes hours, consistently, over a couple of weeks. One slow day means nothing. A steady drift over time is the actual signal, because it usually tracks with how much mental space you're taking up in their day.

This is also the single hardest thing to judge from memory — most people wildly over- or underestimate how fast someone used to reply, because we don't naturally track timestamps. If you've got the actual export, look at the real numbers instead of your recollection of them.

## 2. Who's initiating has quietly flipped

Count the last 20 conversations. Who started them? If it used to be roughly 50/50 and it's now 90/10 in your favor, that's not a mood — that's a real shift in who's doing the reaching-out. This one's genuinely hard to notice in the moment because it happens gradually, one conversation at a time, and you're the one still initiating so it doesn't *feel* like anything changed on your end.

## 3. Message length collapsing

Not every short reply means something — "lol yeah" is a completely normal response to a joke. What's worth noticing is when *every* reply gets short, including to things that used to get a real response: a question about their day, something you're excited about, a story you're telling. When effortful topics start getting one-word answers across the board, that's a genuine drop in investment, not just a texting-style quirk.

## 4. Double-texts going unacknowledged

Everyone double-texts sometimes. What's worth tracking is whether it's becoming a pattern — you send a follow-up because the first message sat unanswered, and it becomes routine rather than the exception. If you're the one closing almost every conversation gap, that's a real, countable pattern, not a feeling.

## What *doesn't* mean anything

- **A single bad week.** Life happens. A busy stretch at work, a family thing, low sleep — none of this is about you, and it resolves.
- **Their texting style, full stop.** Some people are naturally terse texters who are still fully present in the relationship. Compare them to *themselves* over time, not to how you'd prefer someone to text.
- **One unanswered message.** Everyone misses one. The pattern is what matters, not the incident.

## Why "just track it in your head" doesn't actually work

Here's the uncomfortable part: humans are bad at this specific kind of pattern recognition. We remember the exceptions (the one great conversation last Tuesday) more vividly than the trend (three weeks of shrinking replies), and anxiety makes us worse at it, not better — you either fixate on nothing or miss the real thing because you're too close to it.

This is exactly the kind of question that's genuinely better answered by actually counting than by guessing — who initiates, how reply speed has moved over time, how message length has changed. Not to replace your own judgment, but to give it something solid to stand on instead of a feeling you can't fully trust.
