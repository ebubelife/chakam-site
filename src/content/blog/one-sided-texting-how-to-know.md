"I feel like I'm always the one reaching out" is one of the most common relationship complaints there is — and also one of the hardest to actually verify, because feelings about effort are notoriously unreliable in both directions. People who are genuinely carrying a conversation often *underestimate* how one-sided it's gotten, because they're used to it. And people who text plenty but not quite as much as their partner often *overestimate* how unbalanced things are, because their own effort feels effortful, and the other person's doesn't, since you never see what it costs them.

So before "who tries harder" turns into a fight neither of you can actually win with vibes alone, here's what the question actually breaks down into.

## Effort isn't one number — it's at least four

When people say "effort," they're usually blending together several different things that don't always move together:

- **Who starts the conversation.** If it's almost always the same person, that's a real, countable pattern — not a feeling.
- **Who replies faster.** A slow-but-engaged replier and a fast-but-checked-out replier can look identical if you're only tracking "did they respond," not how long it took.
- **Who asks questions and follows up.** Effort isn't just responding — it's showing curiosity, not just answering.
- **Who leaves things unanswered.** The flip side of asking questions is *answering* them. Someone who consistently lets questions sit is showing you something specific, whether or not they mean to.

Two people can be "equally effortful" on one of these and wildly lopsided on another. That's exactly why "it feels one-sided" is both a real signal worth taking seriously *and* a genuinely hard thing to pin down without actually looking at the pattern.

## Why memory alone gets this wrong

Here's the actual mechanism: the messages you send feel effortful because you're the one sending them — you remember composing them, deciding what to say, waiting for a reply. The other person's messages don't come with that same weight in your memory; they just arrive. This isn't a character flaw, it's just how attention works. It means almost everyone's gut sense of "who's trying harder" is quietly biased toward "me," regardless of who's actually initiating more.

The reverse also happens: someone who's genuinely dropped off can convince themselves they're still "putting in effort" because they remember the good conversations vividly and don't notice the growing gaps between them.

Neither side is lying. Both are just running on a system — memory — that isn't built to track rates and ratios accurately. It's built to track what felt significant, and "I sent a message" always feels more significant from the inside than "I received one."

## What actually settles it

Not a longer conversation about who feels more unappreciated — an actual look at the pattern: who initiates, over a real stretch of time, not just the last rough week. How reply speed compares, not just whether someone replied at all. Whether questions are getting genuine engagement or getting left on read.

None of this replaces talking to the person you're texting. But going into that conversation with an actual pattern instead of two competing feelings tends to go a lot better — because "I've noticed I've started most of our last dozen conversations" lands very differently than "it feels like you don't care," even when both are pointing at the same real thing.
